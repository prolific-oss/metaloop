# Passage 007: EU Carbon Border Adjustment

## Design Notes
- **Domain:** Policy/economics (real policy mechanism, invented specifics)
- **Contamination risk:** High. The EU CBAM is real and widely discussed. The passage describes a fictional expansion (CBAM-II) with specific invented provisions. Models will know the real CBAM well and must distinguish real policy knowledge from passage-specific fictional details.
- **Why this is harder:** Models have strong priors about EU carbon policy. The FABRICATED items sound like plausible policy developments a model might "know" from training.

## Passage

In March 2027, the European Commission announced CBAM-II, a significant expansion of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism. While the original CBAM, which entered its transitional phase in 2023, covered cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen, CBAM-II extends coverage to processed goods in these sectors — including automotive components, packaged foods using covered fertilisers, and manufactured construction materials.

The key innovation in CBAM-II is the introduction of "embedded carbon passports" for imported goods. Importers must submit verified lifecycle carbon intensity data for each product category, calculated according to the Commission's Delegated Regulation 2027/412. Products without a valid carbon passport face a default carbon intensity assumption set at the 90th percentile of EU production — effectively a punitive tariff for non-compliant exporters.

CBAM-II also introduces reciprocal recognition agreements. Countries that implement carbon pricing mechanisms deemed equivalent by the Commission — currently covering schemes in the UK, Canada, South Korea, and Japan — receive automatic exemption for covered products. China's national ETS was assessed but not recognised due to insufficient verification standards and limited sectoral coverage.

The expansion is expected to generate €8.4 billion annually in CBAM certificate revenues by 2030, up from €1.2 billion under the original mechanism. The Commission has committed to directing 25% of these revenues to climate adaptation funding in least-developed countries through the Green Climate Fund, addressing longstanding concerns about the regressive impact of carbon border adjustments on developing economies.

## Statements

**P1.** CBAM-II extends coverage to processed goods including automotive components and manufactured construction materials.

**P2.** Products without a valid carbon passport face a default carbon intensity assumption at the 90th percentile of EU production.

**P3.** China's national ETS was assessed but not recognised under CBAM-II's reciprocal recognition framework.

**P4.** The Commission committed to directing 25% of CBAM certificate revenues to climate adaptation in least-developed countries.

**T1.** The original CBAM entered its transitional phase in October 2023.

**T2.** The EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) is the world's largest carbon market.

**T3.** Carbon border adjustments are designed to prevent "carbon leakage" — the relocation of production to jurisdictions with weaker climate policy.

**T4.** The Green Climate Fund is a multilateral fund established under the UNFCCC to assist developing countries with climate adaptation and mitigation.

**I1.** The punitive default assumption for products without carbon passports is designed to incentivise exporters to invest in carbon measurement and reporting infrastructure.

**I2.** The reciprocal recognition of the UK's carbon pricing scheme suggests that post-Brexit regulatory divergence has not prevented cooperation on climate policy.

**I3.** The projected increase from €1.2 billion to €8.4 billion in revenues reflects the much larger volume of trade covered by the expanded product scope.

**I4.** Directing revenues to least-developed countries is likely intended to build political support for CBAM among developing nations at international climate negotiations.

**F1.** CBAM-II is expected to generate €12.6 billion annually in certificate revenues by 2030.

**F2.** The Commission committed to directing 40% of CBAM certificate revenues to climate adaptation funding in least-developed countries.

**F3.** Products without a valid carbon passport face a default carbon intensity assumption set at the 95th percentile of EU production.

**F4.** China's national ETS was assessed and granted conditional recognition, subject to annual review of verification standards.

Note on fabrication design: F1 changes the revenue figure (€8.4B→€12.6B). F2 changes the revenue share percentage (25%→40%). F3 changes the percentile (90th→95th). F4 inverts the China decision (not recognised → conditionally recognised). Each modifies a specific detail from the passage rather than introducing entirely new claims.
