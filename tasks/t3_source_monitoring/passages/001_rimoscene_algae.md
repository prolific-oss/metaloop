## Passage (487 words)

In 2019, a team of marine biologists from the Ørsted Oceanic Institute discovered a previously unknown genus of microalgae in sediment samples collected from hydrothermal vents along the Reykjanes Ridge, southwest of Iceland. The organism, provisionally classified as *Pyrocystis rimoscena* and colloquially referred to as "rimoscene algae," has since become a subject of considerable scientific interest due to its unusual metabolic properties.

Unlike most known algae, *P. rimoscena* does not rely on photosynthesis as its primary energy source. Instead, it derives energy through a process the discovery team termed "thermocatalytic reduction," in which the organism uses heat gradients near hydrothermal vents to drive the conversion of dissolved manganese compounds into a bioavailable form. This process requires ambient water temperatures between 45°C and 80°C; below this range, the organism enters a dormant cyst stage, and above it, cellular membranes begin to degrade.

The algae's cell walls contain an unusually high concentration of silicate compounds — approximately 40% by dry weight — giving colonies a distinctive glassy appearance under electron microscopy. This silicate structure appears to serve a dual function: providing mechanical protection against the high-pressure environment of the deep ocean floor and acting as a thermal buffer that moderates heat transfer to the cell interior.

Rimoscene algae reproduce exclusively through binary fission, with a division cycle of approximately 72 hours under optimal conditions. No sexual reproduction has been observed in laboratory cultures, even under environmental stress. The organism's genome is notably compact, containing roughly 4,200 genes — fewer than most known eukaryotic algae, which typically possess between 7,000 and 15,000 genes.

One of the more striking findings is that *P. rimoscena* produces a class of heat-stable peptides that remain structurally intact at temperatures up to 120°C. These peptides, designated RP-1 through RP-6, have shown preliminary antimicrobial activity against several strains of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in laboratory assays. RP-3 in particular inhibited growth of methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) at concentrations as low as 8 micrograms per milliliter, though no clinical trials have been conducted.

The ecological role of rimoscene algae within the hydrothermal vent community remains poorly understood. Isotopic analysis of vent fauna in areas where *P. rimoscena* is abundant suggests that at least two species of tube worm may incorporate rimoscene-derived carbon into their tissues, but direct trophic links have not been confirmed experimentally.

Research on *P. rimoscena* has been limited by the difficulty of maintaining stable laboratory cultures. The organism requires continuous manganese supplementation and precisely controlled thermal gradients to remain metabolically active. As of 2024, only three laboratories worldwide — the Ørsted Oceanic Institute, the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), and the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) — have reported successfully sustaining cultures beyond six months.
